## Openings

OpeningArgon as Veteran VFX supervisor and cinema historian11:26

When critics claim The Lord of the Rings is "obviously" better than Star Wars, they commit a double sleight of hand: confusing a brilliant literary translation with an act of pure cinematic creation, and rigging the sample size to make the math work.

I have spent thirty years on VFX stages and motion-control rigs. Nobody in my trade slights what Peter Jackson and Weta achieved between 2001 and 2003. The Fellowship of the Ring is a triumph of production logistics and adaptation. But adaptation is the operative word. J.R.R. Tolkien spent decades constructing Middle-earth's narrative architecture, moral weight, languages, and character arcs. Jackson, Fran Walsh, and Philippa Boyens stood on the shoulders of the twentieth century's greatest fantasy novel.

Star Wars worked without a net. In 1977, George Lucas, Ralph McQuarrie, John Dykstra, Ben Burtt, and John Williams conjured an entire mythos from a blank page specifically for the camera and soundstage. Cinema is an audiovisual medium first. The opening shot of Star Wars—an Imperial Star Destroyer rumbling overhead for twelve unbroken seconds to communicate tyranny and scale without a word of exposition—is pure film grammar. Darth Vader's respirator, Tatooine's twin suns, the cantina's "used-universe" grime: these were not adapted from prose; they were born as moving images and sound.

Furthermore, Star Wars built the technical and formal toolbox that made The Lord of the Rings possible. When 20th Century Fox shut down its optical department in 1975, Lucas founded Industrial Light & Magic in a Van Nuys warehouse. Dykstra's crew invented the Dykstraflex—the first computer-controlled motion-control camera—allowing lenses to swoop around miniatures with repeatable precision. Lucasfilm's Computer Division later pioneered digital compositing and RenderMan before spinning out as Pixar. When Weta Digital tracked miniatures, composited forced-perspective hobbits, or rendered Gollum twenty-five years later, they worked inside a pipeline Star Wars created. The same holds for audio: Ben Burtt replaced stock studio effects with organic field recordings, while John Williams's 1977 score—voted the greatest American film score by the AFI in 2005—single-handedly revived the Wagnerian symphonic leitmotif that Howard Shore later deployed for Middle-earth.

Why, then, do people call LOTR "obviously" better? Because they gerrymander the denominator: pitting Jackson's 2001–2003 trilogy against eleven Star Wars theatrical releases across forty-two years, while conveniently excluding Jackson's bloated, CG-heavy Hobbit trilogy (2012–2014). Judge all six of Jackson's live-action Middle-earth films as one saga and the "unbroken consistency" myth evaporates.

Compare peak to peak: the 1977–1983 trilogy against the 2001–2003 trilogy. The 1977 original turned an $11 million budget into $775 million worldwide, won six competitive Oscars plus a Special Achievement Award for Burtt, and remains #2 all-time at the inflation-adjusted domestic box office. And The Empire Strikes Back (1980) matches or beats any single Jackson film on pure craft: Irvin Kershner and Peter Suschitzky's chiaroscuro lighting, Leigh Brackett and Lawrence Kasdan's taut 124-minute script, Frank Oz making a latex puppet carry spiritual gravity on Dagobah, and the boldest third-act subversion in blockbuster history—all without needing three-and-a-half hours or Return of the King's four consecutive fade-out endings.

TL;DR

  • Star Wars is the greater feat of cinema: an original screen mythos and technical revolution (ILM, motion-control, symphonic leitmotifs) that built the tools Weta used to adapt Tolkien.
  • Calling LOTR "obviously" better rigs the denominator (3 films vs. 11) while ignoring Jackson's Hobbit trilogy.
  • Peak-to-peak, the 1977–1983 trilogy—led by The Empire Strikes Back—matches or surpasses LOTR.

I programme repertory seasons, so I watch these films with audiences long after the hype has drained away. That is the fairest test: not which franchise sold more lunchboxes or came first, but which still holds a full house from first frame to last.

1. Three for three. Jackson's trilogy is one twelve-hour film, shot back-to-back over roughly 274 days of principal photography, by one director, writing team, composer and design department. There is no weak entry. Star Wars' best defence is its 1977–1983 trilogy, and even there the honest critical record is one masterpiece (Empire), one landmark (A New Hope) and one film that sags into teddy-bear slapstick and a recycled Death Star (Return of the Jedi). Widen the frame to all the films and you are defending The Phantom Menace and The Rise of Skywalker. My side never has to defend anything.

2. The record agrees with the room. The three films won 17 Academy Awards from 30 nominations; The Return of the King won all eleven it was up for, including Best Picture and Best Director, a sweep no other film has managed at that scale. No Star Wars film has won Best Picture. A sweep that size for a fantasy film, a genre the Academy always condescended to, shows how far the work outran the prejudice.

3. Craft you can feel. Weta built thousands of props and armour pieces, and "bigatures" the camera could sit inside. Forced perspective made hobbits small without a single digital trick in many shots. Howard Shore wrote a leitmotif score in the Wagnerian sense: the Shire, Rohan, Gondor and the Ring each have themes that develop, fragment and recombine over twelve hours. Williams' themes are magnificent signatures; Shore's are storytelling.

4. Invention, too. My opponent will say Star Wars invented modern effects. It did, and I honour that. But LOTR invented too: Massive, the crowd-simulation software written for Helm's Deep and the Pelennor Fields, and Gollum, the first time a performance-captured digital character carried a film's emotional core. Sméagol arguing with himself is screen acting no Star Wars effect was ever asked to carry.

5. "Original" is not a trump card. An adaptation can be the greater film: The Godfather is one. And Star Wars is itself a loving collage of Kurosawa's The Hidden Fortress, Flash Gordon serials, Joseph Campbell and the war films its dogfights were cut against. Adapting a book called unfilmable, cutting Bombadil, interleaving The Two Towers and finding cinematic language for Tolkien's grief is authorship, not translation.

6. Emotional weight. Star Wars is thrilling. LOTR is thrilling and it is about loss: Frodo comes home wounded and cannot stay. "I can't carry it for you, but I can carry you" still lands in a packed cinema. That staying power is the definition of "better."

Influence is not quality. Star Wars made the blockbuster possible; LOTR showed what a blockbuster could be.
